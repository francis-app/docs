COGS moves with revenue more closely than any other P&L line. The right forecasting method depends on how variable your cost structure is and how much granularity the model needs.

## Driver-based

The default for variable direct costs. Add a [formula](/documentation/features/modeling/formulas) to the COGS row that references your revenue group and applies a cost percentage. When revenue assumptions change, COGS updates automatically.

```typescript
= "Revenue"[0] * "COGS % of Revenue"[0]
```

For unit-based forecasting — where cost is driven by volume × unit cost rather than a revenue percentage — the same driver logic applies with different inputs. If unit costs vary by product line or are tied to inventory movements, build a supporting sheet and reference the result into the COGS row.

## Trend-based

Project forward from historical COGS actuals. Add a [formula](/documentation/features/modeling/formulas) to the COGS row referencing a prior period — for example, the same month last year with a small adjustment:

```typescript
= "COGS"[-12] * (1+5%)
```

This takes the COGS value from 12 months ago and applies a 5% increase. If month-to-month actuals are volatile, a rolling average smooths the baseline:

```typescript
= AVG_LAST("COGS", 3) * (1+5%)
```

Use trend-based when your cost structure is stable and the near future is expected to mirror recent history. It becomes unreliable when costs have shifted structurally — a new supplier, a change in product mix, or a step-change in volume.

## Where to forecast COGS

- **Directly on the P&L:** right for most cases. A percentage-of-revenue formula or trend projection lives on the COGS row itself. Simple to set up, easy to maintain.
- **Supporting sheet:** right when COGS is driven by multiple inputs — unit economics, inventory levels, multiple product lines with different margin profiles. The sheet handles the complexity; the P&L row references the result.

If you're unsure, start directly on the P&L. A supporting sheet is straightforward to add later once the model's complexity justifies it.
