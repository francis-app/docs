Loans sit on the liability side of the balance sheet and represent outstanding principal owed to lenders. Common examples include term loans, mortgage facilities, and shareholder loans.

Balance sheet forecasting is always a function of last period's value, additions, and detractions. See [BS fundamentals](/masterclasses-new/budgeting-forecasting/bs-forecasting/bs-fundamentals). For loans, this means loan balance last period + new draws in period − principal repayments in period.

## What approach to use

Hardcoded is the right default. Loan terms are contractual: draws, repayment schedules, and maturity dates are known in advance.

Use driver-based for amortizing loans where repayments follow a formula, or for revolving credit facilities where drawdowns depend on the cash position.

Statistical is not applicable to loans. The balance is determined by contract terms, not historical patterns.

## Where to forecast loans

- **Directly on the balance sheet:** right for most cases. Draws, repayments, and the roll-forward live on the loan balance row.
- **Supporting sheet:** right for revolving facilities or multiple loan tranches with different terms and repayment schedules.

## Forecasting approaches

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Driver-based">
    For amortizing loans with equal monthly repayments, drive the repayment from the loan amount and term:

    ```typescript
    = "Loan amount"[0] / "Loan term (months)"[0]
    ```

    For revolving credit facilities, link the drawdown to the cash position or a minimum cash balance assumption on an assumptions sheet.

    **Impact on cash flow**

    Loan draws and principal repayments sit in financing activities on the cash flow statement. Interest payments sit in operating activities. Keep these separate. Mixing principal and interest in the loan balance is one of the most common errors in three-statement models.

    <Note>Interest is not a repayment. It flows to the P&L as a finance cost and does not reduce the loan balance. Only principal repayments affect the balance sheet.</Note>
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Statistical">
    Statistical forecasting is not applicable to loans. Draws and repayments are contractual; historical patterns don't predict future loan activity.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Hardcoded">
    Hardcode draws in the month the funds are received and repayments directly from the loan schedule. For a bullet loan, set repayments to zero for all periods except the final month.

    For multiple loan tranches or complex repayment schedules, use a supporting sheet that lists each facility with its draw amounts, repayment schedule, and maturity date.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## FAQ

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="How do I model a bullet loan?">
    A bullet loan has no principal repayments during the term. The full balance is repaid at maturity. Set repayments to zero for all periods except the final month, where you hardcode the full outstanding balance.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="How do I handle a loan with a variable repayment schedule?">
    Use a supporting sheet that lists the repayment amount for each period, then reference it into the balance sheet row. This keeps the schedule easy to update if terms change.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
