Decide your forecasting approach for each line item before you start building. It saves rework later. Francis has three approaches, and most models combine all three.

## Driver-based

Forecast as a function of underlying assumptions, called drivers, rather than projecting from history. Drivers map to things people in the business actually understand and control: headcount plans, sales metrics, payment terms. When an assumption changes, everything connected to it updates. That transparency is what makes driver-based models useful for scenario planning.

It's the most aspirational approach, since it reflects how finance teams think about causality, not just historical patterns. It also requires you to define your assumptions and have good data on them.

## Statistical

Forecast as a function of historical patterns. The simplest forms are last year's average plus a YoY growth rate, a rolling average, or holding values flat. More sophisticated forms account for trend, seasonality, and effects from another line item.

Francis offers a function for this purpose: `PREDICT()`. It takes trend, seasonality, and a driver-like effect from another line item into account automatically. You don't need to model seasonality by hand or guess whether a 3- or 6-month rolling average fits best.

## Hardcoded

Hardcode the estimates. No formula, no driver, just a value. Sometimes called "dead numbers" because nothing flows through when assumptions elsewhere change.

The right use case is a known one-off event at a specific time: a big accountant bill in August, a one-time consulting engagement, an annual audit fee. The number doesn't repeat or scale, so there's nothing for a driver or trend to learn from. For recurring line items, hardcoded values are fast to set up but become a maintenance problem. If you hardcode, use formula notes to document the reasoning.

## Choosing between the three

Driver-based is often preferred because it models causality between drivers and the line item. As a general pattern, the biggest items on the P&L, balance sheet, and cash flow warrant driver-based forecasting. These are typically items such as revenue, headcount, receivables, and VAT. There's also a case where driver-based matters more than usual: when the business is changing in ways history doesn't capture, for example startups or new business areas.

Statistical is the better fit when the series is mostly pattern-based or when your data isn't granular enough for a clean driver. Smaller items like many OPEX categories, or static items like many balance sheet items, are fine on statistical.

Hardcoded is the right choice for known one-off events at specific times, the fallback when there's no signal worth modeling, or a starting point during onboarding.

The trade-off is effort and data. A driver model is more to build, and only as good as the drivers you pick and the data you have to estimate them.

## Hardcoded inputs are often statistical in disguise

A common pattern: a department head submits hardcoded numbers, but estimated them as "last year's average plus 5%." That isn't really a hardcoded input. It's statistical forecasting written down without the logic.

When this comes up, model it as an explicit function instead. The logic becomes transparent, scenarios become possible, and the next forecast cycle doesn't require a manual update.

## Starting hardcoded, migrating over time

Finance teams onboarding to Francis often want to copy-paste their Excel budget straight in. That's fine. Hardcoded values get reporting running quickly, which is usually the fastest path to value. Once reporting is in place, migrate the larger line items to driver-based or statistical. The first cycle after migration is where the work pays off.
